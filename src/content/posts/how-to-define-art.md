How should art be defined? I think this is a question everyone who encounters or uses art ends up asking. I had several discussions about it back when I was studying philosophy as an undergrad. I remember my answer back then was that everything in this world is art, because everything is an act of creation, whether or not it is meant as artistic expression. Put differently, if you subjectively consider something to be art, and you can explain it through meaning, then it is art. (Think of all those absurd things displayed in contemporary museums, like a toilet or a banana peel.) Others take the opposite view: that art doesn't need to carry any meaning and is simply for cultivating one's character, an "extra" taste or hobby that sits outside of life. Oscar Wilde wrote in the preface to *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1891): "All art is quite useless." Even the cognitive psychologist Steven Pinker (1997) sees music as an evolutionary byproduct, "auditory cheesecake." Others, from a sociological angle, see art as a way of passing on culture, an intangible asset accumulated over generations, like what Bourdieu (1986) called "cultural capital." Others take an evolutionary view and argue that art has a function. Darwin (1871) proposed the idea of sexual selection, suggesting that human music might have evolved for courtship, like birdsong, and the psychologist Geoffrey Miller (2000) argues that art is like a peacock's tail, a way of displaying one's abilities to attract a mate. I think this may explain part of it, but it's incomplete, since a lot of art has little to do with attracting a mate. Whether or not you think art needs meaning or a particular purpose, today I want to look at it from a more universal, fundamental angle: the biological basis of why art appeared at all.

## Art as a Biological Act

Within the Western framework, art is often defined as whatever is displayed, performed, or exhibited in museums. But Ellen Dissanayake (2008) argues that art is something deeply **biological**. Art exists to give us a sense of control in a life full of uncertainty. Art is an action, and the act of making art, which she calls "making special," is itself an adaptation that evolved to reduce stress.

She gives the example of the Trobriand Islanders. Their ceremonial seafaring canoes (*masawa*), used for long-distance Kula exchange, are carefully carved; ordinary fishing boats get only rough decoration; and personal everyday canoes have no carvings at all. The farther and more uncertain the journey, the higher the anxiety, and so the more "special" the canoe becomes. The carving helps people soothe their anxiety about the unknown, and has little to do with beauty or showing off.

Research on communities around the world has also looked at what happens in the body when people sing or dance together. Tarr (2014) calls this "behavioral synchrony": it triggers endorphins and, through music, supports individual well-being and social bonding. This explains why musical gatherings appear in so many ancient and traditional cultures, like the work songs of West African communities, the harvest festivals of Taiwanese Indigenous peoples such as the Amis, or the San of the Kalahari in southern Africa, where a whole community gathers around a fire, singing, clapping, and dancing through the night in a healing ritual.

All these cases show that art has always been necessary. It is closely tied to individual well-being, and the act of making art appeared long before any definition of art did.

## Why We Create

This also explains why I started making music: it really does make me feel better. It explains why some people who are struggling are almost addicted to creating, and why some workaholics can't stop throwing themselves into any kind of art. In that act they find a bit of control, and with it, less anxiety.

It reminds me of *Rewire* by Nicole Vignola (2024), a book on neuroplasticity. She writes that labeling emotions can reduce anxiety or fear. When we feel anxious or afraid, the amygdala in the limbic system is highly activated. But naming those emotions activates the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, bringing the more rational part of the brain into play, giving us more control over the emotions and easing the feeling of uncertainty.

So I see a strong connection here. Much of making art is about turning human anxiety and uncertainty into an actual symbol, and in doing so, easing that anxiety. Humans spend most of their time acting to reduce uncertainty. But having walked this path myself, I think art also reaches a level beyond controlling uncertainty. Once the need for control settles, a search for something higher begins to stir. Control is one part of it. The other part is almost its opposite.

As a Mandarin speaker, I find defining "health" and "well-being" a little confusing, because I don't think Chinese has a word that matches "well-being" precisely. The direct translation is 福祉, but I don't think it really captures the word. My own understanding is that a person fully develops body, mind, and soul, and these three carry us toward the same place in life. Sometimes what the brain tells us fights with what we feel inside, creating contradictory feelings that leave us unsure what to do. It's a strange phenomenon, and a lifelong lesson. We need to take care of our bodies, understand how our psychological states work, and, if we have the chance, explore the divinity within. All of these matter. And making art may be one of the most important bridges toward this balance of body, mind, and soul. As Richard Hickman (2016) suggests, art is an essential biological behavior for psychological health, because creating and conferring aesthetic significance is an instinctive human desire.

## When Art Goes Beyond Control

I believe art driven by the need for control reflects one of humanity's most basic motivations, and many existing works may come from that motive, as a release or a way of soothing. But I believe art also contains a dimension that goes beyond control.

Take the Tibetan sand mandala (*dul-tson-kyil-khor*, "mandala of colored powders"). Monks spend days or even weeks in full concentration, laying grains of sand one by one into a vast, precise design, and then sweep it away themselves once it is finished. Part of the sand is given to those present as a blessing, and the rest is poured into a nearby river to carry the blessing to all sentient beings, expressing a worldview of impermanence and emptiness (Bryant, 1992).

Or the Japanese shakuhachi practice of *suizen*, "blowing Zen," where playing is itself meditation, in pursuit of *ichion jōbutsu*, enlightenment through a single tone (Blasdel, 2005). In *4'33"*, John Cage let go of control over the notes and created a space of silence, letting the breathing, coughing, and ambient sounds of everyone present become the piece itself, a way of truly sensing the moment (Cage, 1961).

These forms of art all lean toward the other end from control. They focus on letting go and on the present moment, and by giving up control entirely, they can reach a state of self-forgetting.

In my own life as a performer, the feeling of completely giving myself over is like nothing else. My whole body fills with electricity, my sense of self dissolves, and pure energy flows in the present, connecting deeply with others in a form beyond language. This is especially clear in musical performance, because sound waves let everyone in the room share the same vibrations and fall into similar feelings. For me, "reducing anxiety" alone can't explain why I perform. It feels more like a search for the divine.

Research offers some clues. Dietrich (2004) proposed the "transient hypofrontality" hypothesis: in flow, the prefrontal functions responsible for analysis and self-monitoring are temporarily reduced. Later researchers have linked flow to reduced activity in the default mode network (van der Linden et al., 2021), a network tied to self-consciousness and anxiety. When this network quiets down and we let go of control, the boundaries of the self begin to dissolve. And the depth I reach together with an audience connects back to Tarr's behavioral synchrony.

Of course, some might ask whether this, too, is just another way humans respond to the unknown and to uncertainty. I think that question may remain hard to settle. But as I said earlier, if making art is a bridge that brings body, mind, and soul into alignment, then what it can do won't stop at reducing anxiety.

## A Curse in How We Teach Art

Coming back to the practical side, I would say some ways of teaching art, including elite education systems and highly competitive environments, are a "curse" on this instinctive expression. High pressure suppresses a person's natural impulse to create, and ironically, many people have to relearn how to create freely as adults. But I believe we can still find ways to repair this: to discover the true essence of art, release its true potential, and reconnect it with people and with all things. Art is part of our instinctive behavior, far beyond entertainment or competition.

This topic could go much further, but let me sum up here. Back to the opening question: how should art be defined? I would now say that art is the act of making things special. It grows out of our biological need to ease uncertainty and to connect with one another, and over generations it has accumulated into many cultural forms. At the same time, it can become one of the paths that take us beyond self-consciousness and bring body, mind, and soul together. Compared with my old view that "everything is art," this definition is more specific, and it reaches wider than a purely biological explanation.

Biology explains why art came to exist, but it can't fully explain what art can become in the moment. This bridge toward the balance of body, mind, and soul runs in two directions. It grounds us, giving us a little control in a world that is uncertain from the very start. But it also reaches the other way, toward something transcendent and sacred, a place words can't reach. There, the self dissolves, and we enter flow, a kind of emptiness, and a search for the divine.
